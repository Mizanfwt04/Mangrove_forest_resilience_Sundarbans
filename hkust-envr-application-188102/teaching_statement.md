## 1. Teaching philosophy

Environmental challenges cannot be solved by science alone, nor by technology without ecological understanding. My teaching philosophy rests on three principles:

1. **Theory first, application always.** Students must grasp ecological mechanisms—how biodiversity supports ecosystem services, how ecosystems respond to extremes—before evaluating nature-based solutions or carbon projects. Rigor and relevance are not in tension.

2. **Experiential, evidence-based learning.** I believe in “hands-on” instruction: field measurements, remote-sensing labs, data analysis, and case studies drawn from real landscapes. Students should leave a course able to *do* science, not only describe it.

3. **Interdisciplinary fluency.** ENVR students come from engineering, business, science, and the social sciences. I design courses so that a future engineer, financier, or policy analyst can understand how biodiversity and NbS connect to their professional choices—mirroring the hub’s mission and the 2027 biodiversity core curriculum.

## 4. Pedagogical methods

**Field-based learning.** Short exercises on tree identification, biodiversity metrics, soil/sediment sampling, and coastal observation—followed by lab analysis. HKUST’s unique managed coastline is an ideal teaching asset.

**Data-centric labs.** Students work with real satellite imagery, LiDAR point clouds, and ecological datasets using Python/R and GEE. Assignments emphasise reproducibility and open science.

**Case-study seminars.** Hong Kong examples (mangroves, artificial shorelines, urban trees, country parks) alongside tropical case studies from my research—highlighting transferable principles.

**Inclusive assessment.** Mix of quizzes, field reports, group projects, policy memos, and oral presentations so students with different strengths can succeed.

**Guest practitioners.** Invite AFCD, NGO, and industry partners to connect classroom concepts to implementation—consistent with ENVR’s applied mission.
